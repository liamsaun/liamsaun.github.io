Low Cost Prosthetics

Proof of concept for affordable prosthetic limbs using 3D printing and modular design, aimed at making assistive technology accessible to underserved communities.

CAD Design 3D Printing Biomechanics Arduino SolidWorks Prototyping

Overview

Traditional prosthetic limbs can cost anywhere from $5,000 to $50,000, making them inaccessible to millions of people worldwide who need them. This project explores how modern 3D printing technology and modular design principles can drastically reduce manufacturing costs while maintaining functionality and comfort.

The goal was to create a proof of concept for a prosthetic hand that could be manufactured for under $20 in materials, with assembly time under 24 hours, making it viable for widespread distribution in developing regions and underserved communities.

The Problem

Over 40 million people worldwide need prosthetic limbs, but less than 10% have access to them due to high costs and limited availability. In developing countries, this problem is even more severe, with prosthetics often costing more than a year's income for the average family.

"How can we leverage accessible technology to create functional prosthetics that don't compromise on quality while remaining affordable?"

Design Process

Research & Analysis

The project began with extensive research into existing prosthetic designs, biomechanics of hand movement, and 3D printing materials. I studied both commercial prosthetics and open-source designs to understand what makes them effective and where costs could be reduced.

CAD Modeling

Using SolidWorks, I designed a prosthetic hand with the following key features:

  • Adjustable sizing to fit different hand sizes without complete redesign
  • Replaceable finger components for easy maintenance and repair
  • Simplified mechanical linkages for reliable grip functionality
  • Optimized geometry for 3D printing with minimal support material
  • Ergonomic socket design for comfortable long-term wear

Prototyping & Testing

Multiple iterations were printed using PLA materials to test durability, fit, and functionality. Each prototype was evaluated for:

  • Structural integrity under typical use loads
  • Range of motion and grip strength
  • Comfort during extended wear periods
  • Ease of assembly and adjustment
  • Cost-effectiveness of materials and print time

Technical Implementation

The design utilizes a cable-driven mechanism for finger articulation, allowing for a simple and effective grip. One change I would make in the future iterations is to add sensor integration for nerve signals to control the prosthetic hand more intuitively. This would involve incorporating myoelectric sensors that can detect muscle movements in the residual limb, allowing for more natural control of the prosthetic fingers.

Key technical achievements include:

  • Grip force of up to 25 lbs, sufficient for most daily tasks
  • Individual finger articulation for improved dexterity
  • Total material cost under $20 for complete assembly
  • Print time of 24 hours for all components

This proof of concept demonstrates that functional prosthetics can be produced at a fraction of traditional costs without sacrificing essential functionality. The modular design also means that damaged components can be easily replaced, extending the lifespan of the device.

Challenges & Learning

Throughout this project, I encountered several significant challenges:

  • Balancing strength requirements with weight constraints for comfortable wear
  • Optimizing the mechanical advantage of the cable system for effective grip
  • Ensuring reliable 3D printing across different printer types and settings
  • Creating adjustable components that maintain structural integrity
  • Understanding the biomechanics of natural hand movement

Each challenge taught me valuable lessons about engineering design, user-centered thinking, and the importance of iterative prototyping. The experience reinforced my passion for thoughtful engineering and its potential to create meaningful impact.

Future Developments

While this proof of concept demonstrates feasibility, there are several areas for future improvement:

  • Integration of myoelectric sensors for intuitive muscle-controlled operation
  • Advanced materials testing for improved durability and weather resistance
  • User studies with actual amputees to gather feedback and refine design
  • Development of companion software for easy customization and sizing
  • Partnership with organizations to pilot distribution in underserved regions

This project remains an important example of my design experience, while my current academic focus is Chemical Engineering and the broader challenge of improving materials, processes, and manufacturing systems.
